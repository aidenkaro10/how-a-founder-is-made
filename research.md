# Background Research: Why Is the Israeli Startup Ecosystem So Effective?

*TAMID Group, Israel Connection Project. Research checked October 2026.*

## The short answer

Israel has about 10.3 million people [1]. That's smaller than New York City plus its suburbs. But it has more than 7,200 startups listed on Startup Nation Central's database [2], and it spends a bigger share of its economy on research than any other OECD country [4]. No single thing explains this. It's a stack of things that hit a kid at every stage of life: a blunt culture, growing up with real pressure, a military that hands teenagers huge responsibility, waves of skilled immigrants, strong universities, and a government that kick-started venture capital and then got out of the way.

## Culture

Israelis are known for chutzpah. The word comes from the Hebrew for "insolence" or "audacity" [16]. In Yiddish it was an insult. In startup talk it means nerve, like asking for the meeting you have no right to ask for. There's also "dugri," which means talking straight. Researcher Tamar Katriel wrote a whole book on it in 1986, describing it as the blunt speaking style of native-born Israelis [15]. In practice this means people tell you your idea is bad, to your face, and that's normal.

Failure isn't a dead end either. A 2018 World Economic Forum report called Israel the place where "entrepreneurial failure is most accepted" [31]. IEEE Spectrum tells the story of Modu, a phone startup that raised over $120 million and shut down after three years. Its ex-employees went on to start dozens of new companies [32].

## Growing up with pressure

Kids in towns near Gaza grow up with the "Tzeva Adom" (Red Alert) siren. In Sderot, residents have about 15 seconds to reach cover after it sounds [13]. Since 1993, after Iraq fired Scud missiles at Israel during the 1991 Gulf War, the law has required new homes to include a reinforced safe room, called a mamad [14]. Kids learn early to stay calm and move fast.

There's also Yom HaZikaron, Israel's Memorial Day. A siren sounds across the whole country for one minute the night before and for two minutes at 11 a.m. Traffic stops, and drivers get out of their cars on the highway and stand in silence [33]. Kids grow up knowing that in a few years it's their turn to serve, and that weight makes a lot of them serious early.

Kibbutz kids get it too. Drip irrigation, now used on farms all over the world, became a company on a kibbutz. Netafim was set up in 1965 at Kibbutz Hatzerim in the Negev, built on engineer Simcha Blass's dripper, and Hatzerim still owns part of it [34][35].

Kids also get responsibility early in youth movements. In these groups, teens aged about 15 to 17 can become counselors (madrichim) and run activities for younger kids [17]. The Israeli Scouts (Tzofim), the biggest movement, say they have around 100,000 members [18].

## The military

Military service is required for most Jewish Israelis at 18. Men serve 32 months and women serve 24 months [6][7]. During the war that started in 2023, the army pushed to make men serve 36 months. In July 2026 the Knesset kept it at 32 instead [6].

Some units work like tech schools. Unit 8200, the signals intelligence unit, is the largest single unit in the IDF and is often compared to the U.S. NSA [8]. Its alumni founded companies like Check Point, ICQ and NICE [9]. All four Wiz founders served there too [11]. Talpiot is even more selective. It started in 1979 because the 1973 Yom Kippur War showed Israel needed a tech edge. It takes no more than 60 people a year out of roughly 10,000 top math and physics students [10]. Fun fact: Check Point's co-founder Marius Nacht came out of Talpiot, not 8200 [10].

The culture matters as much as the tech. In "Start-Up Nation" (2009), Dan Senor and Saul Singer argue that the IDF runs with a flat hierarchy where junior soldiers are expected to challenge officers [12]. One review quoted on the publisher's page puts it simply: authority "not only can be challenged, but must be" [12]. The book also describes "rosh gadol" (big head), meaning doing what the mission actually needs, not just the literal order, and a habit of debriefing everything, wins and mistakes [12]. After the army, reserve duty (miluim) continues for years, usually up to around age 40 [7]. Units stay together for decades, so the people you served with become a lifelong network [12].

Right after the army, a lot of Israelis take "the big trip." A 2013 survey by the travel agency Issta, cited by the Times of Israel, estimated up to 40,000 Israelis go backpacking abroad each year. That's about a third of the roughly 75,000 who finish service annually, and India is a classic stop [19]. Months of figuring things out on your own in a foreign country is basically founder training.

## Immigration and education

Between 1990 and 1998, about 879,000 people moved to Israel, mostly from the former Soviet Union. That grew the population by 19% in under ten years. Over 55% had post-secondary education, compared with about 25% of native-born Israelis, and about 15% were engineers or architects [20]. The Times of Israel says this wave "fed Israel's nascent tech scene" with skills it badly needed [21].

The universities each have a different superpower:

- **Technion** (Haifa) ranked 97th in the world in the 2025 Shanghai ranking [22]. Three of its professors have won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, in 2004 and 2011 [23].
- **Tel Aviv University** ranked 7th in the world, and 1st outside the U.S., for producing VC-backed founders in PitchBook's 2025 ranking. Its alumni started 736 companies in a decade [24].
- **Ben-Gurion University** (Beer Sheva) anchors a tech park next to campus that hosts CyberSpark, a cyber cluster built with the government's cyber agency and the city. The site sits next to the planned base for the IDF's elite tech units [25].
- **Hebrew University** professor Amnon Shashua co-founded Mobileye in 1999 out of his own research [26].
- **Weizmann Institute** set up Yeda in 1959. It was Israel's first technology transfer company, built to turn lab discoveries into products [27].

## Government and capital

In 1993 the government launched Yozma. It put in $100 million to back ten new private VC funds of about $20 million each. Each fund needed a foreign VC partner. The government took a 40% stake, and private partners could buy it out after five years if things went well. Most did. By 2000, Israel's VC market was almost fully private [5]. Today, VC investment hit 2.3% of GDP in 2020, and foreign investors supplied 71% of startup funding from 2015 to 2023 [5].

That foreign money ties into one more thing. Israel's home market is tiny, so startups are "born global" and aim at the U.S. and Europe from day one [28].

## Big tech, the US, and universities

**Big tech moved in.** Intel opened a small development center in Haifa in 1974, its first outside the US [36]. Microsoft followed in 1991 with its first R&D center outside the US, also in Haifa. Bill Gates said it started because Israeli engineers at Microsoft wanted to move home [37]. By 2024, Startup Nation Central counted 434 multinational R&D centers in Israel. They employ about a third of the country's tech workforce and account for 40% of its R&D spending [38].

**The US connection.** The US and Israel set up the BIRD Foundation in 1977 with $60 million, $30 million from each country, to fund joint projects between American and Israeli companies [39]. BIRD says it has approved close to 1,200 projects, with more than $10 billion in sales; other sources put it around 1,000 projects [40]. In 1985 Israel signed the first free trade agreement the US ever made [41]. About 111 Israeli companies were listed on Nasdaq as of January 2025 [42].

**From lab to company.** The Weizmann Institute set up Yeda in 1959 [27], and Hebrew University set up Yissum in 1964 [43]. Copaxone, Teva's multiple sclerosis drug, was discovered at Weizmann by Michael Sela and Ruth Arnon and licensed to Teva through Yeda [44]. Hebrew University research also gave the world long-life cherry tomatoes, plus the Alzheimer's drug Exelon and the cancer drug Doxil [43].

## Results

Israel spent 6.8% of GDP on R&D in 2024, the highest in the OECD, where the average is 2.7% [4]. The exits are huge. Google bought Waze in 2013 for a reported $1.1 billion [29]. Intel bought Mobileye in 2017 for about $15.3 billion [30]. In March 2026, Google closed its $32 billion deal for Wiz, the biggest exit in Israeli history [11]. In 2025, Israeli startups raised about $15.6 billion and M&A deals added up to $74.3 billion [3].
